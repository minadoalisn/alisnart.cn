How Long Does a Custom Light Sculpture Actually Take?

Short answer: plan in months, not weeks. A mid-size custom piece runs through site checks and design, a few weeks of fabrication, shipping, and an install finished with a night commissioning. The exact length depends on size, site readiness, and one decision most buyers postpone too long: freezing the design.

When someone asks me for a date in the first meeting, I give them a range and a condition. The range is honest. The condition is the site facts. A schedule built before we know the ground, the power, and the access is not a schedule. It is a wish.

Fabrication is rarely the part that slips

People picture the workshop as the bottleneck. In my experience it is not. Once steel is cut and parts are ordered, fabrication moves at a steady pace — a few weeks for a mid-size piece, longer as size and moving parts grow. What slips is everything around it: waiting on a site answer, reworking a detail that was never settled, or arriving on site to find the power is not ready.

The question I ask first on any new job has nothing to do with the lights: where does the truck stop, and where does the crane stand? If nobody can answer that, the install date is fiction. I have seen installs lose days to a gate that was too narrow and a ground surface that would not take a crane. Both were knowable in week one.

The five stages, in plain terms

First, the brief and the site facts. Photos, dimensions, power points, wind exposure, water depth if it sits near water, and the date that cannot move. This stage takes days when the answers come back fast, and weeks when they trickle in one email at a time.

Second, design and freeze. This is where the look, the structure, and the control approach get settled together. Freeze means freeze: no more shape changes after this point without accepting a new date. The most expensive sentence in this business is "just one small change" after steel is cut.

Third, fabrication. Structure, skin, lighting, and control come together in the workshop, and the piece gets tested as a whole before it is packed — not for the first time on your site, at night, in front of an audience.

Fourth, shipping. A piece ships in sections, and the section sizes are decided by the truck and the site access, not by what looks tidy on a drawing. Packing for a long sea trip is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Fifth, install and night commissioning. The piece goes up in daylight and gets judged at night. That last part matters more than people think: sign-off should happen at night, from the main viewing angle, with the program running. A daylight sign-off is how a piece opens looking flat.

Three ways to lose a month

Late site data is the first. Every week the site answers are late, the whole chain shifts, because design cannot freeze on guesses.

Changes after the freeze are the second. A new shape means new steel, new skin, and sometimes a new control layout. It is not a tweak. It is a restart of the affected parts.

Power not ready on install day is the third, and the most avoidable. The install crew should arrive to a tested supply, not to a trench that is still being dug. Confirm the supply before the truck leaves, not after it arrives.

What to send first

If you want a schedule that means something, send these before asking for a date: site photos and dimensions, the power points and their capacity, truck and crane access, wind and water conditions where they apply, and the one date that cannot move. With those five things, a fabricator can tell you what is realistic. Without them, any date you get is a guess dressed up as a plan.

FAQ

Q: How long does a custom outdoor light sculpture take from brief to switch-on?

A: Plan in months, not weeks. A mid-size custom piece typically runs through site checks and design, a few weeks of fabrication, shipping, and an install plus night commissioning. The exact length depends on size, site readiness, and how early the design is frozen.

Q: What delays a custom light sculpture project most often?

A: Late site facts, design changes after steel is cut, and power not being ready on install day. Fabrication itself rarely slips; the gaps between stages do.

Q: When should the design of a light sculpture be frozen?

A: Before steel is cut and parts are ordered. Changes after that point do not just cost money, they restart the clock on the parts that have to be remade.

Q: What should a buyer send first to get a realistic schedule?

A: Site photos and dimensions, power points, wind and water conditions where they apply, truck and crane access, and the one date that cannot move. With those, a fabricator can give a schedule that means something.
