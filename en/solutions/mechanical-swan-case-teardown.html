Taking Apart a Mechanical Swan: What Makes the Show Work

Short answer: a mechanical swan is a 3.5-metre, 150 kg water robot, and the show works when four things hold — position, power, light sync, and a pull-in plan. This page takes the case apart piece by piece, using the numbers from our own swan case page. Nothing here is a guess.

I wrote the buyer FAQ first, because that is the conversation I keep having. This is the second half of it: not what a buyer asks, but what I look at when I open the case up and check whether the thing will still be running on night ten. The swan is the easy part. The show is the hard part.

What is the swan, exactly?

The hull is 3.5 by 1.8 by 1.5 metres, about 150 kg, moulded in one piece from high-strength ASA composite. It handles fresh water and seawater, resists UV and corrosion, and carries an IP68 rating. Service life is rated at five years or more. Two ducted thrusters push it at 0.5 to 1.5 m/s, under 40 decibels at cruise.

The quiet is not a nice extra. A show runs to music, and a hum over the music reads as a fault to everyone on the bank. The hull is the show's body and its boat in one piece, so a scratch in the finish is also a waterproofing question, not a paint question. That is why the hull is one moulded piece instead of panels bolted together: fewer seams, fewer places for water to get in.

How does a formation hold its shape?

Each unit holds position with RTK-GPS to ±0.5 m, and the shore base station that makes that figure true is part of the setup. Four ultrasonic sensors plus two visual sensors watch 0.3 to 5 metres ahead. The cluster control runs from a PC or a tablet app, with comms out to 1,000 metres, and a formation runs from one unit up to 100 or more. The light is RGBW, DMX512/Art-Net, thousands of built-in animations, 200 lumens per bead, synced to music.

The control loop stays local. A formation of 100 units, talking to a shore controller up to 1,000 metres away, cannot wait on a remote server to decide its next frame. The decision about the next move happens on site. Hesitation in front of a live audience reads as a fault, and the audience does not know or care why the swans stopped.

How long does it run, and what eats the battery?

Six to eight hours on one charge, from a 24V/100Ah lithium pack. Cruise speed and light brightness set the exact number. One unit takes about four hours to recharge. A shore charging pile or a battery swap covers the gap between shows. The full breakdown — what eats the battery, and how to test runtime at real show brightness — is on our runtime page.

Test runtime at show brightness, not at idle. Brightness is what eats the pack, and a runtime figure measured with the lights dim is a number for a different show. Low battery sends the unit home on its own. Loss of signal stops it or sends it home. The operator holds a remote emergency stop.

What stops the show?

The published limits are wind above level 5 (10.7 m/s) and waves above 0.5 m. The swan itself runs from −10°C to 50°C. The pull-in call needs a named person and a place for the units to sit out of the water, decided before the first rehearsal — not during a squall, when nobody is making good decisions.

The detail worth rehearsing is the gap. A unit that turns for home mid-formation leaves a hole in the picture. A formation rehearses that hole — where the neighbours close up, and whether the audience even notices. If a supplier has never rehearsed it, they have never run a formation.

What does it cost, and what should the quote include?

A standard 3.5-metre unit runs RMB 12,000–38,000, depending on configuration. A multi-unit formation is quoted per project, by unit count and show complexity. A single swan drops in and runs. A formation takes 7 to 10 days on site: GPS base station, water survey, route planning, formation programming, system integration, and on-site testing.

The question behind the price question is what the quote includes. Ask it directly: does the number cover the shore base station, the charging setup, and the on-site testing days, or are those separate lines? A quote that leaves the base station out looks cheaper and is not. Without the base station, the ±0.5 m positioning figure does not hold, and the formation drifts.

What I check before ordering

Four checks, in this order. Is the shore base station in the quote, or is it a separate line? Does the pull-in plan have a name on it, and a place for the units to sit out of the water? Has the formation rehearsed the hole — one unit going home mid-show? Was runtime tested at real show brightness, not at idle? If a supplier can answer all four, the swan will do its job. The rest is choreography.
