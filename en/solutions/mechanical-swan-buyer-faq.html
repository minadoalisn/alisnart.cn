What Buyers Ask Before Ordering a Mechanical Swan

Short answer: one swan runs 6 to 8 hours, costs RMB 12,000–38,000, and a formation takes 7 to 10 days to set up. The rest of this page is the seven questions every buyer asks before signing, with the numbers behind each answer. Nothing here is a guess — every figure comes from our case page or our factory page, and the links at the bottom go straight to both.

I have sat through this conversation enough times to know the order it goes in. Price first, then runtime, then weather. The order is wrong. Weather should come first, because it decides whether the show happens at all. But nobody asks it first, so here are the questions in the order buyers actually ask them — with the answers I give.

How much does one swan cost?

A standard 3.5-metre unit runs RMB 12,000–38,000, depending on configuration. That range covers the light package, the control setup, and the finish. A multi-unit formation is quoted per project, by unit count and show complexity — there is no flat formation price, because a 12-unit show on a small lake and a 60-unit show across a wide river are different jobs.

The question behind the price question is what the quote includes. Ask it directly: does the number cover the shore base station, the charging setup, and the on-site testing days, or are those separate lines? A quote that leaves the base station out looks cheaper and is not. Without the base station, the ±0.5 m positioning figure does not hold, and the formation drifts.

How many swans make a show?

One swan on its own already works. It drops in and runs. A formation runs from a few units up to 100 or more. The count is a choreography decision, not a status decision: how far the audience stands, how wide the water is, and how dense the pattern needs to read from the bank.

A small lake with the audience ten metres from the water needs fewer units than a wide river viewed from a promenade fifty metres back. The mistake I see most is ordering by headcount — "we want 30 swans" — before anyone has stood on the bank and looked at the distance. Count the viewing distance first. The unit count follows.

How long does it stay out, and what happens when it gets tired?

Six to eight hours on one charge, from a 24V/100Ah lithium pack. Cruise speed and light brightness set the exact number. One unit takes about four hours to recharge. A shore charging pile or a battery swap covers the gap between shows. The full breakdown — what eats the battery, and how to test runtime at real show brightness — is on our runtime page.

Low battery sends the unit home on its own. Loss of signal stops it or sends it home. The operator holds a remote emergency stop. The detail worth rehearsing is the gap: a unit that turns for home mid-formation leaves a hole in the picture. A formation rehearses that hole — where the neighbours close up, and whether the audience even notices. If a supplier has never rehearsed it, they have never run a formation.

Will it hold its place in the pattern?

Each unit holds position with RTK-GPS to ±0.5 m, and the shore base station that makes that figure true is part of the 7 to 10 day setup — GPS base station, water survey, route planning, formation programming, system integration, on-site testing. Four ultrasonic sensors plus two visual sensors watch 0.3 to 5 metres ahead.

The control loop stays local. A formation of 100 units, talking to a shore controller up to 1,000 metres away, cannot wait on a remote server to decide its next frame. The decision about the next move happens on site. Hesitation in front of a live audience reads as a fault, and the audience does not know or care why the swans stopped.

What weather calls it off?

The published limits are wind above level 5 (10.7 m/s) and waves above 0.5 m. The swan itself runs from −10°C to 50°C. The pull-in call needs a named person and a place for the units to sit out of the water, decided before the first rehearsal — not during a squall, when nobody is making good decisions.

This is the question that separates a supplier from a boat builder. A supplier has a pull-in plan with a name on it. A boat builder has a spec sheet. Ask who makes the call, who does the pulling, and where the units go. If the answer is a shrug, the weather will answer it for you on the night.

How loud is it, and how long does it last?

Under 40 decibels at cruise — quiet enough that the show does not hum over the music. Two ducted thrusters push it at 0.5 to 1.5 m/s. The hull is 3.5 by 1.8 by 1.5 metres, about 150 kg, moulded in one piece from high-strength ASA composite. It handles fresh water and seawater, resists UV and corrosion, and carries an IP68 rating. Service life is rated at five years or more.

None of that is glamorous. All of it is what decides whether the tenth night runs like the first. A swan that looks right on night one and leaks on night ten is not a show piece. It is a repair bill with feathers.

How long to set up, and who fixes it if something breaks?

A single swan drops in and runs. A formation takes 7 to 10 days on site: base station, survey, route planning, programming, integration, testing. The testing days are not padding. They are where the formation stops being a drawing and starts being a show.

The full spec sheet for the swan itself is on the swan case page, and the waterfront brief covers depth, anchoring, shore power, and viewing direction for the site itself.
