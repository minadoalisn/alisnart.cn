What Overseas Buyers Ask Before Ordering a Custom Light Installation

Short answer: the render is the easy part. What decides whether a custom light installation succeeds is the site, the shipping, and who can open it up in year two. Here are the questions I get asked most, with the answers I actually give.

Can my site take a light installation?

Send me a photo of the site and I can tell you more than a ten-page brief can. I look at four things: where people enter, the main sightline, where they stop and queue, and the available power points. Fire clearance decides how close a crowd can get. Theme, shape, and color come after all of that.

The question buyers never ask but should: can the delivery truck and a small crane reach the exact spot? If the answer is no, the design changes — not the logistics. We sort out transport segments and the lifting path at the drawing stage, because fixing it on site means improvisation, and improvisation on a crane day is expensive.

How long does it take from drawing to delivery?

Concept and shop drawings usually take a few weeks. Fabrication then runs in parallel sections — steel, wiring, diffusers — so the shop time overlaps instead of stacking.

The honest variable is scale and foundations. A plaza piece on a concrete base needs anchor design and curing time, and no schedule compresses that. If someone promises you a large permanent piece in two weeks, they skipped a step. Ask which step.

How does a big sculpture ship overseas?

In segments. And the segment lines are decided on the drawing, not at the port.

Here is the detail only people who have shipped this stuff know: a standard container door is about 2.3 meters wide. If a piece cannot break down to fit through it, it ships flat-rack at a painful premium. So we draw the segment boundaries before the first piece of steel is cut. Every segment gets numbered drawings so the site crew knows the assembly order without calling us at midnight.

Who installs it on site?

The drawing set does most of the work: anchor design, lifting points, and numbered wiring diagrams. A competent local contractor can set and wire the piece from those — the anchors are engineered, not improvised on the day.

We keep three circuit modes separate on the drawings: normal, festival, and maintenance. That way switching the show scene for a holiday never risks the daily program, and the maintenance crew can kill the artwork circuits while the plaza stays lit.

What if something breaks in year two?

Then the first drawing did its job — or it did not. Service hatch, numbered cables and connectors, drivers in a separate ventilated cabinet, and a small box of spare LED modules plus one spare driver shipped with the project. A local electrician can swap a driver without touching the artwork.

The long version of this answer is in our notes on why outdoor light sculptures go dark in year two. Same rule applies: design the repair before you need it.

What do buyers forget to ask?

"What does the maintenance access look like?" If the first drawing has no service hatch, someone will cut one with an angle grinder in year two, and it will look exactly like that. Ask for it now, on paper, while changes are still free.

And send the site photo first. Everything good starts from the site.
