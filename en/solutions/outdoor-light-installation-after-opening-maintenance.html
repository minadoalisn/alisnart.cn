After the Lights Go Up: Keeping an Outdoor Light Installation Alive

Short answer: opening night is the easy part. Week two is when the piece gets tested — dust on the lenses, a dead driver, a program that reset itself after a power cut. The installations that still look good in month six were designed for that week, not just for the opening photo.

I have seen the same pattern on outdoor work for years. Everyone budgets for the build and the opening, and nobody budgets for the Tuesday night three weeks later when one section goes dark and nobody on site knows which box to open. Maintenance is not a footnote. It is the second half of the design.

Spares on site, not on a courier route

The rule I work to: one spare driver for each driver type in the piece, one spare controller, and the fuses and connectors the design actually uses — in a labelled box on site, before opening. A fault during show week is fixed in an hour when the spare is in that box. It takes days when it has to come by courier, and days during a festival are the days that matter.

Ask your supplier for the spares list in writing, with part numbers. If the list says "standard driver" with no number, you do not have a spares list. You have a hope.

Cleaning: the slow fade nobody notices

Dust, rain film, and pollen dull lenses and diffusers gradually. The trap is that it happens slowly, so nobody notices — the piece looks fine in daylight, and a little tired at night. Then someone compares a photo from opening week with one from now, and the difference is embarrassing.

The check that catches it: look at the piece at night, from the main viewing and photo angle, once a week in the early run. Not from a ladder in daylight. Daylight hides exactly what the audience sees. A wipe-down schedule tied to that night check beats any fixed calendar, because a dusty site and a clean site do not need the same interval.

Service access: design the repair before you need it

Every part that can fail will eventually fail, and a sealed sculpture with no service opening has to be cut open to fix. That is the detail I push hardest in design reviews, because it is invisible in a render and expensive in real life.

What good access looks like: hatches a technician can actually reach without scaffolding, a control cabinet at ground level with labelled keys, and cables routed so one section can be isolated without killing the whole piece. Ask where the driver for the top section lives, and how someone reaches it in the rain. If the answer involves a ladder, a crane, or "we will work that out", the design is not finished.

Back up the program, twice

A programmed piece has a second failure mode that has nothing to do with hardware: the show file. A power cut, a reset, or a well-meaning button press can drop a controller back to its factory default, and suddenly your festival program is a static white glow on opening weekend.

Keep the show file in two places: on the controller, and on a labelled USB kept in the cabinet. Label the version and the date. The five minutes this takes at handover saves the one phone call nobody wants to make at 9 p.m. on a Saturday.

Who owns it after handover

The last question, and the one that decides whether the rest happens: who, by name, owns this piece after we leave? Not a department. A person, with the cabinet key, the spares box, and our phone number. If that person changes, the handover notes need to change with them.

A short maintenance note at handover — spares list, cleaning check, program backup, and the name of the owner — costs almost nothing and decides whether the piece still earns its photos in year two. The build gets the applause. The maintenance gets the repeat visit.

FAQ

Q: What spares should be held on site for an outdoor light installation?

A: At least one spare driver for each driver type in the piece, one spare controller, and the fuses and connectors the design actually uses. A fault during show week is fixed in an hour when the spare is in a box on site, and in days when it has to come by courier.

Q: How often should outdoor light fixtures be cleaned?

A: Often enough that nobody notices the output dropping. Dust, rain film, and pollen dull lenses and diffusers gradually, so the piece looks fine in daylight and tired at night. Check it at night, from the main viewing angle, not from a ladder in daylight.

Q: Why does service access matter in a light sculpture design?

A: Because every part that can fail will eventually fail, and a sealed sculpture with no service opening has to be cut open to fix. Hatches, reachable cabinets, and labelled keys decide whether a repair takes an hour or a rebuild.
